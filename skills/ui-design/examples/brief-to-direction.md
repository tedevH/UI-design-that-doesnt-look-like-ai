# From brief to visual direction

This walkthrough shows how to turn a thin request into a design that has a reason to look the way it does. It is an illustrative exercise, not user research or a claim about conversion.

## The brief

> Make a website for a neighborhood bakery. It should feel warm and make it easy to find today's bread and opening hours.

## 1. Identify the actual decisions

A visitor is trying to answer three questions quickly:

1. Is the bakery open, and where is it?
2. What can I buy today?
3. Is there a practical reason to visit now?

That makes today's bake list, hours, and location more useful than a generic brand story. If the owner has real product photography, it can support the page; if not, a typographic bake schedule or simple flour-mark illustration is more honest than random lifestyle stock.

## 2. Write the direction card

- **Audience and task:** nearby residents choosing whether to stop in.
- **Visual premise:** the rhythm of a small morning bake, presented like a changing paper counter list.
- **First read:** today's open/closed status and signature loaf; then today's bake list; then hours and directions.
- **Composition:** an asymmetric opening with a narrow, oversized bake list beside one tactile bread photograph or a restrained hand-drawn mark. Later sections shift to a compact weekly schedule and practical location details.
- **Visual system:** warm paper background, dark ink, one oven-clay accent; expressive serif for the bread names, plain readable sans for logistics; thin rules and small batch labels used only where they clarify.
- **Responsive shift:** stack the list before the image, keep open status and address near the top, and make directions and call buttons easy to reach.
- **Interaction character:** the schedule is real content, hours are scannable, and directions open a usable map link.
- **Restraint:** no invented awards, fake reviews, decorative icon grid, or faux artisan copy.

## 3. Build and inspect

Implement in the existing framework if one exists. Use the owner's copy and assets; label any sample menu items as placeholders. At desktop, inspect whether the bake list has enough scale to be the focal point. At mobile, check that today's items, open status, and directions survive the rearrangement.

Use `references/quality-bar.md` to critique the rendered page. Fix the first meaningful mismatch. For example, if opening hours are hard to find, moving them into a glowing badge is probably less effective than changing the hierarchy and placement.

## 4. Learn from the result

A useful review note is specific: “I like the paper schedule, but the loaf photo should feel less polished and the hours need to appear sooner.” Record the preference at that level. Do not turn one note into a universal rule such as “always use paper textures” or “never use polished photography.”

## Why this is better than choosing a style label

“Warm crafted” is a useful vocabulary cue, but it does not tell you what the visitor needs, what the page should emphasize, or what changes on mobile. The direction becomes distinctive when the bakery's real content determines the hierarchy and the visual treatment.
