# Visual quality bar

Use this guide to make a design memorable and to critique the rendered result. It is not a scorecard. A page does not need to maximize every dimension; it needs a strong, coherent response to the user's brief.

## The direction card

Before implementation, define the direction in enough detail that another designer could build the same idea without guessing. Keep it to a few lines unless the user asks for a fuller plan.

- **Audience and task:** who this serves and what they need to do or understand.
- **Visual premise:** the distinct idea, tied to real product, content, brand, or context.
- **First read:** the order of attention in the opening view.
- **Composition:** where the focal material sits, the page's alignment logic, and how the scroll changes pace.
- **Visual system:** typography roles, color roles, surfaces, image or illustration treatment, and detail language.
- **Responsive shift:** what changes in layout and hierarchy on narrow screens.
- **Interaction character:** how feedback, motion, and important states should feel.
- **Restraint:** what the page deliberately leaves out.

The direction should be specific enough to reject generic substitutions. “Warm, premium, playful” alone is not a direction. “A neighborhood bakery told through its morning production rhythm: oversized bread schedule, paper-white canvas, ink-blue type, one ochre flour mark” is closer because it names content, hierarchy, material, color, and a visual premise.

## Composition before effects

A beautiful page can be quiet. Look for an intentional relationship between:
- focal scale and surrounding space;
- alignment and deliberate breaks;
- text and image proportions;
- repeated elements and visual variation;
- large areas and fine detail;
- information density and breathing room;
- page rhythm and the user's task.

Do not add blur, shadows, grain, animation, a large illustration, or overlapping cards simply because they are available. Add a treatment only when it strengthens the visual premise or clarifies a state.

## Visual critique pass

Inspect the rendered design at desktop and mobile sizes. Ask these in order:

1. **Purpose:** Can a viewer tell what the page is and what to do?
2. **Identity:** What makes it belong to this particular product?
3. **Hierarchy:** Is the first read clear and correctly prioritized?
4. **Composition:** Are scale, crop, alignment, spacing, and density intentional?
5. **Specificity:** Are the imagery, product details, data, and copy credible for this brief?
6. **Coherence:** Do typography, palette, surfaces, and graphics feel like one design?
7. **Rhythm:** Does the page develop, or repeat one template section?
8. **Responsive behavior:** Does the narrow layout preserve the idea and usability?
9. **Finish:** Are controls, labels, states, contrast, and motion handled?

Fix the earliest serious failure in that sequence. A page with no identity does not need another shadow; a page with poor hierarchy does not need another font.

## Heuristics are prompts, not verdicts

Automated detectors can locate possible problems such as overflow, weak contrast, missing labels, repeated card geometry, clichés, or generic type. They cannot judge brand fit, composition, taste, or beauty. Keep objective accessibility and layout failures separate from subjective style signals.

A centered composition, serif font, bright color, card grid, gradient, animation, or icon library can be right. Review its context and execution before recommending removal. Never change a successful design solely to lower a heuristic count.

## Evidence of quality

When possible, keep examples that show the request, the final rendered screenshots at desktop and mobile, and a short note about the design decisions. Label illustrative demos as examples, not as user research or proof of general performance. For meaningful quality comparisons, hold the prompt and implementation context constant and compare results blind against the same visual rubric.
